My fieldwork in Ethiopia gave me the opportunity to learn directly from people, communities, and agricultural environments beyond what I can observe in survey data. This page shares some of the people, landscapes, crops, and everyday experiences that shaped my understanding of the country.

## Fieldwork and Local Communities

During my fieldwork in rural Ethiopia, I traveled to local kebeles to better understand the conditions behind the patterns I observed in household survey data. I spoke with rural households, local officials, and researchers to learn from their experiences and perspectives on local economic conditions, agricultural production, and changing climate conditions. Being in the field allowed me to observe the environments in which households make decisions and better understand the opportunities and constraints that are difficult to capture through quantitative data alone.

## Agriculture and Climate

Agriculture is central to rural livelihoods in Ethiopia. During my fieldwork, I encountered a diverse range of crops, including maize, enset (false banana), coffee, teff, wheat, cabbage, and sugarcane. This year has been particularly difficult for most of the farmers I met. Farmers described rainfall arriving at the wrong time, disrupting planting, crop growth, and harvesting. In some communities I visited, farmers reported losing much or all of their maize harvest, while enset—an important crop in southern Ethiopia—was also affected by disease.

What struck me equally, however, was how farmers responded. Despite these setbacks, they continued to work hard on their land, adapting their production and planting crops such as potatoes, teff, and beans after losses earlier in the season. Their persistence in the face of difficult growing conditions was particularly striking. At the same time, adaptation extended beyond the farm: some households sent family members to nearby towns for seasonal work, sold livestock to cope with income losses, or started small agriculture-related businesses to diversify their livelihoods. Seeing these responses firsthand gave me a deeper understanding of how climate shocks shape not only agricultural production, but also household labor allocation, livelihoods, and broader economic decisions.

### Food and Culture

Fieldwork also gave me the opportunity to experience Ethiopia through its food and everyday traditions. Many of the foods I encountered were closely connected to the crops and livelihoods I was learning about in the field, from coffee and teff to enset and other locally grown crops. Sharing meals and coffee with local colleagues and friends was also an important part of getting to know the communities where I worked.
