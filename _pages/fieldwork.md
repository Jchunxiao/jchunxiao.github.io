# Fieldwork in Ethiopia

My fieldwork in Ethiopia gave me the opportunity to learn directly from people, communities, and agricultural environments beyond what I can observe in survey data. This page shares some of the people, landscapes, crops, and everyday experiences that shaped my understanding of the country.

## In the Field

During my fieldwork in Ethiopia, I traveled to local kebeles to better understand the economic conditions behind the patterns I observed in household survey data. Being in the field allowed me to learn from local knowledge, observe the environments in which households make decisions, and better understand the opportunities and constraints that are difficult to capture through quantitative data alone.

## Agriculture and Climate

Agriculture is central to rural livelihoods in Ethiopia. During my fieldwork, I encountered a diverse range of crops, including maize, enset (false banana), coffee, teff, wheat, cabbage, and sugarcane. This year's growing season has been particularly difficult for many of the farmers I met. Farmers described rainfall arriving at the wrong time, disrupting planting and crop growth. In some communities I visited, farmers reported losing much or all of their maize harvest, while enset—an important staple crop in southern Ethiopia—was also affected by disease.

What struck me equally, however, was how farmers responded. Despite these setbacks, they continued to cultivate their land and adjust their production, moving on to crops such as potatoes, teff, and beans. Seeing these challenges and responses firsthand gave me a deeper understanding of how climate shocks shape agricultural production, household livelihoods, and economic decisions—the same relationships that motivate much of my research.

### Food and Culture

Fieldwork also gave me the opportunity to experience Ethiopia through its food and everyday traditions. Many of the foods I encountered were closely connected to the crops and livelihoods I was learning about in the field—from coffee and teff to enset and other locally grown crops. Sharing meals and coffee with local colleagues and friends was also an important part of getting to know the communities where I worked.
